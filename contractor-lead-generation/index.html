Contractor lead generation

You don't have a lead problem.You have a delivery problem.

Most contractors who feel starved of work are already paying for enough leads. The leads arrive, ring out, sit in an inbox until Monday, and get booked by whoever called back first. Buying more of them just means losing more of them. This page is about what happens between the lead arriving and the job being signed — and what a lead genuinely costs once you measure it there.

The six leaks

Where do contractor leads actually die?

Almost never at the point of generation, which is where nearly all the money gets spent. They die in the gap between arriving and being spoken to. Every leak below is cheaper to fix than it is to out-spend, and most of them cost nothing but a setting and a habit.

Leak 01

Nobody answered

You were on a roof, under a sink, or driving. The call went to voicemail and the homeowner moved to the next name. This is the single largest leak in the trades and the one most easily closed with a missed-call text-back that already exists in most phone systems.

Leak 02

Somebody else answered first

On a shared platform four other contractors got the same lead at the same moment. Research consistently finds 78% of customers buy from whoever responds first — not the cheapest, not the best reviewed. You paid the same price for a race you did not know you were in.

Leak 03

It arrived after hours

A form submitted at 7pm on a Friday sits until Monday. By then the homeowner has a quote and a start date from someone else. Emergency trades lose the most here, but every trade loses something, and the fix is routing rather than staffing.

Leak 04

It was quoted and then forgotten

A quote that goes quiet is not a lost job, it is a homeowner still deciding — waiting on a spouse, a bonus, or a season. Most contractors chase twice and stop. The follow-up window for bigger work is months, not days.

Leak 05

Nobody knows where it came from

Without call tracking per channel you cannot tell which source produced which customer, so you cannot cut what is failing or feed what is working. Most contractor budgets are allocated on a feeling, and the feeling is usually wrong.

Leak 06

The customer left no trace

Job done, nobody asked for a review, nobody kept the details. That customer produced one payment instead of a ranking signal, a referral and a repeat job. This is the leak that costs the most over five years and gets noticed the least.

Run your own numbers

What does a shared lead actually cost you per booked job?

Several times the sticker price, and the multiplier is how many contractors it was sold to. You are not buying a lead on a marketplace, you are buying a lottery ticket in a race decided by who dials first. Put in what you pay and how often you win that race, and this prices both sides honestly — including the case where the shared lead wins.

The work, in order

How do you plug the leaks before buying more leads?

In this order, because it runs cheapest first. Everything in the first three steps costs less than a fortnight of ad spend and improves every channel at once, including the leads you are not paying for.

Day one · usually already in your phone system

Missed-call text-back

An automatic text within seconds of any missed call. This alone recovers more jobs than most budget increases, because a homeowner who gets an instant reply has not started dialling the next contractor yet. Most systems have it built in and switched off.

Week one

Decide who owns after-hours

Forwarding, a service, or a shared rota. Not "we'll get to it Monday". A form submitted at 7pm on Friday is a live homeowner for about an hour and a cold record by the weekend. The fix is routing rather than hiring.

Week one · free

Track calls by source before anything else

A separate tracking number per channel. Until that exists you are guessing which source produced which customer, and every budget decision after this point depends on it. It is also how you catch a platform charging you for leads you generated yourself.

Week two

Build the follow-up nobody builds

Every quote enters a sequence that keeps you present for months, offering something useful rather than asking whether they are ready. The window on larger work is measured in months, and most contractors chase twice and write the lead off.

Week two · permanent

Ask for the review on your own profile

Every completed job, regardless of where the lead came from. Reviews on a marketplace are that marketplace's asset; reviews on your Google profile feed your map pack position and stay yours. This is how you convert rented leads into owned visibility.

Month two onward

Shift the ratio toward channels you own

Keep buying while the owned side builds — cash flow beats theory. But every month that map position and organic rankings improve is a month you need the marketplace less. The goal is making paid spend optional rather than structural.

What can a contractor fix this week without spending anything?

Four things, and any one of them may beat a budget increase. If you do only these and never call us, this page has done its job.

Turn on missed-call text-back

Check your phone system or CRM. It is almost certainly there and switched off, and it is the single cheapest recovery of leads you have already paid for.

Count last month's leads against last month's jobs

Two numbers on a scrap of paper. If the gap is large, your problem is delivery and no amount of extra lead spend will fix it.

Ask for a Google review on every job

Including the ones that came from a marketplace. That is how you turn a rented lead into an asset that keeps working on a profile you own.

Call every quote from the last three months

Not to ask if they are ready — to tell them something useful. Some of them are ready, and you have already paid for all of them.

Straight answers

Contractor lead generation questions

How much do contractor leads cost in 2026?
Between roughly $15 and $200 per lead for most residential and light commercial work, depending on trade, job size and market. Angi commonly runs $15 to $100 or more per lead on top of an annual membership of around $300 to $500. Thumbtack typically runs $8 to $150 per response, with most trades between $25 and $75 and no annual fee. Google Local Services Ads averaged $53 per lead in February 2026 across 888 contractors. The lead price is the least useful number in that list, because the platforms differ enormously in how many contractors receive the same lead.
What is the difference between shared and exclusive contractor leads?
An exclusive lead is sold to you alone. A shared lead is the same homeowner sold simultaneously to three to five contractors, and sometimes as many as eight. The homeowner submitted one request and receives a wave of near-identical calls, so the job tends to go to whoever reached them first rather than whoever was best suited. That is why cost per lead is misleading in this category: two leads at the same sticker price can differ by a factor of five in what they actually cost you per booked job.
Is Angi worth it for contractors?
As a short-term way to fill a gap in the schedule, sometimes. As a foundation, no. Published analysis puts a $50 shared lead won 20 percent of the time and closed 30 percent of the time at roughly $833 per booked job, and for higher-ticket trades in competitive markets effective acquisition costs of $1,000 to $1,400 per booked job are commonly reported. The contractors who do make it work almost all share one trait: somebody answers within seconds. If your response time is measured in hours, shared leads are the worst possible place to spend money.
What is a good cost per booked job for a contractor?
Under your gross profit per job, and ideally well under. The benchmark worth holding is the published exclusive figure: Local Services Ads averaged $53 per lead at a 43.9 percent book rate in February 2026, which works out to roughly $233 per paying customer against an average ticket of $1,826. Compare any lead source you are considering against that rather than against its own sticker price. A channel with cheaper leads and a worse book rate is not cheaper, and that single confusion explains most contractor marketing budgets that feel like they are not working.
How do I get exclusive contractor leads?
By generating them rather than buying them from a marketplace. Google Local Services Ads are exclusive because the homeowner clicked your listing and called your number. Google Ads leads are exclusive for the same reason. Map pack position, organic rankings and referrals are exclusive and carry no per-lead cost at all once earned. There are also vendors selling genuinely exclusive leads at higher sticker prices, which can work if the cost per booked job stacks up. The distinction that matters is not who sold it to you but how many other contractors are dialling the same number.
Why do my leads never answer the phone?
Often because four other contractors called first. On shared platforms a homeowner submits one request and then fields a wave of calls, and a common reaction is to stop answering entirely. You can pay for a lead and never reach a human being on it. The other frequent cause is timing: research consistently finds that 78 percent of customers buy from the first business to respond, and that contacting a lead within five minutes makes you far more likely to reach and qualify them. If your average response is measured in tens of minutes, the lead was probably alive when it arrived and dead by the time you called.
Do reviews from Angi or Thumbtack help my Google ranking?
No, and this is one of the most expensive misunderstandings in contractor marketing. Reviews you earn on a marketplace stay on that marketplace. They cannot be migrated to your Google Business Profile, they do not feed your map pack position, and if you leave the platform they leave with it. Contractors who use marketplaces sensibly still ask every completed customer for a Google review regardless of where the lead came from, precisely so the reputation accrues to an asset they own rather than one they rent.
Should contractors buy leads or generate their own?
Buy when the schedule has a hole and you need work this fortnight, generate for everything after that. Bought leads start immediately and stop the day you stop paying, and the price is set by somebody else. Owned channels take six to twelve months to carry real weight and then produce leads with no per-lead cost while the underlying asset keeps appreciating. The mistake is treating them as alternatives rather than as different time horizons. Run both, and shift the ratio deliberately as the owned side matures.
