How to get more Google reviews as a contractor, without breaking the rules

Ask every customer, the same way, at the moment they're happiest with the work, and hand them a link that opens your review form in one tap. That's most of it. The rest is doing it every time, and staying clear of the shortcuts Google and the FTC now actively punish.

Why do Google reviews matter so much for contractors?

Because they affect both whether you show up and whether you get chosen. Google says in its own ranking guidance that more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking, and once a homeowner sees three contractors on the map, your rating, your count and the date of your newest review are most of what they have to compare.

Reviews also feed the paid side. Google's Local Services Ads help lists your rating and number of reviews as part of profile quality, which is one of the inputs to where your ad shows. And when someone asks an AI assistant who to hire, the answer leans heavily on review data and your Google profile; we cover that in how contractors get recommended by ChatGPT.

The frustrating part is that most contractors with ten reviews have hundreds of satisfied customers. The gap isn't quality of work. It's that nobody asked, or they asked once, vaguely, while packing up the truck.

What do Google and the FTC prohibit?

No incentives for reviews, no asking only the happy customers, no pressure on site, no fake or family reviews, and no threats to get a bad review removed. Break those and you risk reviews being removed, a warning on your profile, or worse.

Google's Maps user-contributed content policy spells out what businesses can't do. The parts that trip up contractors most:

  • Incentives. No payment, discount, gift card, free service or raffle entry in exchange for a review, positive or negative. Reviews posted because of an incentive get removed. Google's own help page for businesses calls this strictly prohibited.
  • Review gating. Sending a "how did we do?" survey and only pointing the 5-star answers at Google is selectively soliciting positive reviews, which the policy bans. A private check-in is fine. The Google request has to go to everyone.
  • Pressure on site. Businesses shouldn't require or pressure customers to review while on the premises. For a contractor, the homeowner's kitchen counts. Handing someone your tablet and waiting is pressure.
  • Quotas and scripts for staff. Telling crew leads to collect a certain number of reviews, or to ask customers to mention them by name, is now listed as prohibited.
  • Conflicts of interest. Reviews from employees, former employees, family or business partners don't belong on your profile.

The FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials, announced in August 2024, adds federal teeth: it bans fake and AI-written reviews, incentives conditioned on a positive (or negative) review, and using threats or intimidation to suppress negative ones, and it lets the FTC seek civil penalties. The practical takeaway is simple. Ask honestly, ask everyone, give nothing for it.

When should a contractor ask for a review?

At the walkthrough, when the work is finished, the site is clean and the homeowner is looking at the result. That's the moment of peak satisfaction on almost every job, and it fades fast once the invoice shows up.

How that plays out depends on the trade:

  • Service calls (plumbing, HVAC, electrical). Same day. Mention it before you leave, and the request lands on their phone within the hour.
  • One- to three-day jobs (roofing, painting, fencing). At the final walkthrough, then the written request that evening, not after payment clears a week later.
  • Long projects (remodels, additions, pools). At substantial completion, before punch-list fatigue sets in. If the final payment is likely to involve a tense conversation, sort that out first. A review request on top of a dispute helps nobody.

One timing rule holds everywhere: ask while the job is still the most recent thing they've spent money on. Three weeks later you're competing with their dentist and their car mechanic for the same goodwill.

Exactly what should you say?

Two parts: a short, human ask in person, and a written request with the link. The in-person part sets it up so the message doesn't feel random. Neither part should mention stars, a rating or specific words.

In person, at the walkthrough

"We're a small company and most of our work comes from people reading reviews. Would you be willing to write a few honest lines on Google about how this went? I'll text you the link tonight so it's one tap."

The text or email, same day

"Hi Pat, thanks again for having us out for the water heater today. If you have a minute, an honest review helps other homeowners decide who to call: [your review link]. Thank you, Luis, Front Range Plumbing."

Get your direct link from your Google Business Profile: Google's help page shows how to share a review link or QR code that opens the review form directly. Put that QR code on the leave-behind folder, the invoice and the business card your tech hands over. Every extra step between "sure" and the review box loses people.

How do you turn asking into a routine?

Make one person responsible, trigger the request from the job being marked complete, send one reminder, and count the results monthly. Good intentions lose to a busy week; a routine doesn't.

  1. Pick the trigger. Job marked complete in your scheduling software, or the final invoice sent. Whatever it is, every job hits it.
  2. Send the request automatically from that trigger, under your company name, with the one-tap link.
  3. Remind once, about three days later, if no review has appeared. Then stop. Repeated nagging is how a 5-star customer becomes a 3-star one.
  4. Count monthly: requests sent, reviews received, average rating. If requests go out and nothing comes back, the ask or the timing is off.
  5. Never filter. The customer who had a rough start gets the same request as everyone else. If something went wrong, call them first and fix it; don't skip them.

How many reviews is enough? There's no number Google publishes. Look at the three companies in your trade that appear most for your main search in your town, note their counts and how fresh their newest reviews are, and aim to pass them steadily. A slow, steady stream looks like a busy, current business. A burst of thirty in a week looks like a campaign.

Should you ask for reviews on Yelp, Facebook or Angi too?

Put Google first and keep the request to one link. Asking for reviews on several sites in the same message splits a small number of willing customers across platforms, and one of the big ones, Yelp, tells businesses not to ask at all.

Yelp's own support page is blunt about it: don't ask anyone to review your business, whether customers, friends or a mailing list, and don't ask right after a survey or feedback form. Its software tends to filter reviews that look solicited into the "not recommended" section, where they don't count toward your rating. So the practical approach on Yelp is to claim your page, keep the details accurate, reply to what comes in, and let it grow on its own.

Facebook recommendations are worth having if your customers actually use Facebook to find contractors in your area, but they rarely move the needle on search the way Google reviews do. Reviews left on a lead marketplace mostly stay on that marketplace, which is the subtle cost we cover in is Angi worth it for contractors: you are building someone else's profile. If you only have the energy for one request per customer, make it Google.

Should you reply to every review?

Yes, briefly and specifically. Google's advice is to keep replies short and relevant, skip the promotions, and respond to negative reviews constructively and promptly. "Thanks, Pat. Glad the new water heater is keeping up with three teenagers" reads like a real company. "Thank you for your 5-star review! We appreciate your business!" pasted forty times does not.

Common questions

Can I offer a discount or gift card for a Google review?
No. Google's content policy prohibits offering payment, discounts or free goods or services in exchange for any review, positive or negative, and says reviews posted because of an incentive will be removed. A raffle entry or a discount on the next service counts. Thank customers for the work, not for the review.
Can I send happy customers to Google and unhappy ones to a private form?
No. That is review gating, and Google's policy prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews. You can absolutely check in privately and fix a problem, but the request to leave a Google review has to go to every customer the same way, regardless of how you think they feel.
How many Google reviews does a contractor need?
There is no magic number. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking, and homeowners compare you against the other businesses on the same map. Look at the three companies in your trade that show up most for your main search in your town, note their review counts and how recent their newest reviews are, and aim to pass them steadily rather than in a burst.
