What should a contractor read first?

Start with the lead cost post if you're deciding where to put money, the Angi one if you're currently buying shared leads, and the Google Maps one if you already know something is wrong and can't work out what. Each is written to stand alone, so there's no reading order to follow.

Decision Lead buying

Is Angi worth it for contractors?

The honest answer depends on one number most contractors have never worked out: cost per booked job, not cost per lead. A $50 shared lead sold to four contractors, won 20% of the time and closed 30% of those, costs $833 per job. We run that arithmetic properly, cover the membership fees, the dispute process, what happens to reviews you earn on the platform, and the situations where buying shared leads is genuinely the right call — because there are some.

Local Services Ads vs Google Ads: which should a contractor run?

They sit on the same results page, charge on completely different models, and answer different questions. Local Services Ads charge per lead, carry the Google Verified badge and book at 43.9%; Google Ads charge per click, cost $90.92 per lead on average, and reach searches LSAs cannot. We cover eligibility, what the licence verification actually involves, when running both makes sense, and the dispute process almost nobody uses that quietly inflates their real cost per lead.

Pick the right one

Which post answers the question you actually have?

Each of the five exists for a different situation, and reading the wrong one first wastes your evening. Here's who each is written for — including who should skip it, because not every post is useful to every contractor.

Read 01 if

You're deciding where to put money

You have a budget and several options and no reliable way to compare them. The lead cost post puts every published 2026 benchmark side by side so you can see that a $53 Local Services Ads lead and a $90.92 Google Ads lead are not actually comparable until you know the book rate behind each. Skip it if you already know your cost per booked job by channel — you're ahead of the post.

Read 02 if

You're currently buying shared leads

You're on Angi, Thumbtack or similar and can't tell whether it's working. The post runs the arithmetic most contractors never do: a $50 shared lead won 20% of the time and closed 30% of those costs $833 per booked job, which changes the comparison entirely. Skip it if you've never used a lead marketplace and don't intend to.

Read 03 if

You're about to start paid ads

Or you're running one and wondering about the other. Covers eligibility, what licence verification involves, the different charging models, and the honest answer on running both. Skip it if your break-even cost per lead is under about $40 — the transfer test will tell you that in thirty seconds, and paid search probably isn't your route.

Read 04 if

You want the least contested visibility available

AI assistants are recommending contractors right now and roughly 1.2% of local businesses appear in those answers, which means the barrier is attention rather than budget. Most of the work is free. Skip it if your Google Business Profile is still incomplete — do that first, since it feeds this anyway.

Read 05 if

Something is wrong and you can't tell what

You know you're not appearing where you should be. The post orders the nine causes by likelihood rather than alphabetically, which matters because the most common one is proximity and no amount of work fixes it. Skip it if you rank well throughout your service area already — but check from more than one location before you conclude that.

All five are written to stand alone, so there's no order to follow and no series to work through. Where one references a figure explained properly in another, it links there rather than repeating the explanation badly.

How this blog works

Why are there only five posts?

Because publishing on a schedule produces filler, and filler is worse than nothing — it dilutes the pages that do work and wastes the time of the person reading it. We add a post when there's something to say that isn't already said better elsewhere. That happens a few times a year, not weekly.

Rule 01

Every number is sourced and dated

If a figure appears, the source is named and so is when it was published. Marketing statistics rot fast, and an undated benchmark is a guess with a decimal point in it.

Rule 02

Ranges, not single numbers

Where two credible sources disagree we show both rather than picking the flattering one. Where a range is wide we say why — usually trade, market, season or execution.

Rule 03

No gated content

Nothing here requires an email address. No newsletter, no download form in front of a PDF, no "unlock the full guide". If it's written, it's readable.

Rule 04

We'll say when a channel isn't worth it

Including channels we're paid to run. The Angi post is the clearest example — it names the situations where buying shared leads is the correct decision, which isn't the answer an agency is supposed to give.

Rule 05

Written for contractors

Not for other marketers, and not for a search algorithm. If a term needs explaining it gets explained or linked to the glossary rather than assumed.

Rule 06

Corrections stay visible

When a figure turns out to be wrong or a source is superseded, the post is updated and the change is noted rather than quietly edited. The review date on each post is real.

There's no newsletter signup on this page and there won't be one. If you want to know when something new goes up, the honest answer is to check back occasionally or call us — we'd rather have a conversation than an open rate.

There's a commercial argument for this as well as an editorial one, and it's worth stating plainly because it explains the whole approach. Search engines and AI assistants both reward depth and specificity over volume, and a site carrying five thoroughly sourced posts will generally outperform one carrying fifty thin ones — partly because thin pages compete with each other for the same terms, and partly because nobody cites a page that restates what three other pages already said. The posts here are written to be quoted: dated figures, named sources, one question answered properly per post. That's the same reason the glossary carries a benchmark on every definition and the expectations page shows ranges rather than a single promised number. It is also, incidentally, exactly what we'd tell you to do with your own site.

Straight answers

Questions about this blog

How often do you publish new posts?
Rarely, and deliberately so. We publish when there is something worth saying that is not already said better somewhere else, which works out at a few times a year rather than weekly. Publishing on a schedule produces filler, and filler is worse than nothing because it dilutes the pages that do work and wastes the reader's time. Five well-sourced posts that answer real questions will outperform fifty written to fill a content calendar, both for the reader and in search.
Do I need to give an email address to read anything?
No. Nothing on this site is gated, there is no newsletter, and there is no download form sitting in front of a PDF. Every calculator runs in your browser without sending anything anywhere, and every post is readable in full. If you want to know when something new goes up, check back occasionally or call us on 1-800-481-8638. We would rather have a conversation than an open rate.
Where do the benchmark figures come from?
Named, dated, published sources, and they are cited on the page where they appear rather than gathered in a footnote nobody reads. The recurring ones are the SearchLight Digital Local Services Ads dataset from February 2026, covering $6.72 million in tracked spend across 888 contractors; LocaliQ's home services and healthcare benchmarks across thousands of campaigns; ServiceTitan response time research; USPS postage schedules; and published conversion data from WebFX and PipelineOn. Where two credible sources disagree we show both rather than choosing the flattering one.
Can I use these figures in my own marketing or reporting?
Yes, and we would rather you cited the original source than us. Every figure names where it came from precisely so you can go and check it, which is the point of dating them. If you are quoting a benchmark to a client, a supplier or an agency you are evaluating, go to the primary source and confirm it is still current. Marketing statistics rot quickly, and a number that was accurate in February may not be by December.
Will you write about my trade specifically?
Possibly, though the trade pages on this site already cover roofing, plumbing, HVAC, electrical, solar, general contracting, painting, remodeling, pool building and fencing in more depth than a blog post would. The blog exists for questions that cut across trades, like what a lead costs or whether a platform is worth buying from. If there is a question specific to your trade that is not answered anywhere on the site, email info@eyetoad.com and it is genuinely useful to know.
Do you accept guest posts or link exchanges?
No to both. Guest posts written to place a link are the reason most industry blogs are unreadable, and link exchanges are a practice search engines have discounted for years while continuing to carry risk. If you have data worth publishing we would rather cite you properly from a post we wrote ourselves, which is more useful to a reader and safer for both sites.
Why are the posts so long?
Because the questions they answer are not simple, and a short answer to a complicated question is usually a wrong one. Whether Angi is worth it genuinely depends on your average job value, your close rate, how fast you answer the phone and what else you are running, and any post that gives a straight yes or no is either guessing or selling something. The posts are structured so you can read the section that applies to you and stop, with the arithmetic shown rather than asserted so you can substitute your own numbers.

Reading is cheaper than testing. Both beat guessing.
